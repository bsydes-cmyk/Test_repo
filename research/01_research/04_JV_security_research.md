# Real Estate Joint Venture LLC: True JV vs. Securities Offering Analysis

**Date:** May 8, 2026
**Subject:** Whether a member-managed real estate JV LLC with active participants is an "investment contract" under federal and state securities laws
**Deal:** $1.9M acquisition, $650K equity, 6–8 active members, equity split 50/15/35 (capital/raise/sweat)

---

## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

- **The default rule**: A general partnership or member-managed JV LLC interest is **presumptively NOT a security** because each partner has the legal power and economic incentive to protect themselves. This presumption was established in *Williamson v. Tucker*, 645 F.2d 404 (5th Cir. 1981), and is followed nationwide.
- **The presumption is rebuttable** under *Williamson*'s three factors: (a) agreement leaves partners with limited power, (b) partners are inexperienced/unknowledgeable, or (c) partners are dependent on a unique, irreplaceable promoter. Satisfying any one factor flips the JV into a security.
- **Your structure is favorably positioned**: 6–8 members, every member with a defined operational role, a small ratio of members to active roles, and personal guarantees from members signal real participation, not Howey-style passivity.
- **Principal red flags to neutralize**: (1) the "capital-raising via personal network" 15% slice can look like commission-based syndication marketing; (2) any acquisition fee or promote that flows disproportionately to organizers re-introduces a sponsor/investor dynamic; (3) one member with non-replaceable expertise collapses the JV into a security under Williamson factor 3; (4) general solicitation kills any "private placement" backstop.
- **Belt-and-suspenders backup**: Even if confident the JV is not a security, structuring the offering so it would *also* satisfy Section 4(a)(2) / Rule 506(b) costs almost nothing and provides litigation insurance.

## 1. THE DEFINITION OF "SECURITY" — STATUTORY ANCHOR

**Securities Act of 1933, § 2(a)(1) (15 U.S.C. § 77b(a)(1))** defines "security" to include "any note, stock, treasury stock, security future, security-based swap, bond, debenture, evidence of indebtedness, certificate of interest or participation in any profit-sharing agreement, … **investment contract** …"

The "investment contract" prong is the only catchall that LLC interests typically fall under.

---

## 2. THE HOWEY TEST — VERBATIM

**SEC v. W. J. Howey Co., 328 U.S. 293, 298–99 (1946)**:

> "[A]n investment contract for purposes of the Securities Act means **a contract, transaction or scheme whereby a person invests his money in a common enterprise and is led to expect profits solely from the efforts of the promoter or a third party**, it being immaterial whether the shares in the enterprise are evidenced by formal certificates or by nominal interests in the physical assets employed in the enterprise."

### Plain-English four prongs

1. **Investment of money** — capital contribution, signing a PG, or pledged sweat.
2. **Common enterprise** — circuits split between "horizontal commonality" and "vertical commonality." A multi-member LLC pooling capital almost always satisfies horizontal commonality.
3. **Expectation of profits** — economic returns, not consumption.
4. **Solely from the efforts of others** — softened to "**predominantly**" or "**essential managerial efforts which affect the failure or success of the enterprise**" (*SEC v. Glenn W. Turner Enterprises*, 474 F.2d 476 (9th Cir. 1973)).

**The fourth prong is where the JV-is-not-a-security analysis lives.**

---

## 3. WILLIAMSON v. TUCKER — THE THREE FACTORS

**Williamson v. Tucker, 645 F.2d 404, 424 (5th Cir. 1981)**:

> "**A general partnership or joint venture interest can be designated a security if the investor can establish, for example, that (1) an agreement among the parties leaves so little power in the hands of the partner or venturer that the arrangement in fact distributes power as would a limited partnership; or (2) the partner or venturer is so inexperienced and unknowledgeable in business affairs that he is incapable of intelligently exercising his partnership or venturer powers; or (3) the partner or venturer is so dependent on some unique entrepreneurial or managerial ability of the promoter or manager that he cannot replace the manager of the enterprise or otherwise exercise meaningful partnership or venturer powers.**"

### Factor-by-factor

**Factor 1 — Power Distribution** ("de facto limited partnership" test): Look at the operating agreement. Does each member actually have voting rights, removal rights, information rights, and approval rights over major decisions?

**Factor 2 — Inexperience/Lack of Knowledge**: Are the members capable of meaningfully exercising their rights? Your members each holding a defined operational title with apparent expertise cuts strongly against Factor 2.

**Factor 3 — Dependence on Unique Promoter**: Even if power is distributed and members are sophisticated, if there is one person with non-replaceable expertise, the others "depend solely on his efforts" and the interest is a security.

---

## 4. PROGENY — HOW COURTS APPLY WILLIAMSON

### *SEC v. Merchant Capital, LLC*, 483 F.3d 747 (11th Cir. 2007)
The Eleventh Circuit adopted the *Williamson* framework and **flipped a registered LLP (RLLP) into a security**. Key holdings: court analyzes "the expectations of control **at the time the interest is sold**."

### *Long v. Shultz Cattle Co.*, 881 F.2d 129 (5th Cir. 1989)
Cattle-feeding investors signed a "consulting agreement" with the promoter. A **management/consulting fee paid to the organizer** is evidence that the organizer is the "real" manager.

### *Robinson v. Glynn*, 349 F.3d 166 (4th Cir. 2003)
The Fourth Circuit held that a $25M LLC member interest **was NOT a security** because the member had **substantial managerial control**:
- Treasurer of the LLC
- Member of the seven-person Board of Managers
- Member of the executive committee
- Veto power over indebtedness outside the ordinary course

> "[Robinson] carefully negotiated for a level of control 'antithetical to the notion of member passivity' required to find an investment contract under the federal securities laws."

### Other relevant authority

## 5. REGULATION D / FORM D BACKDROP

If the interest **were** a security:
- **Section 4(a)(2)** — exempts "transactions by an issuer not involving any public offering."
- **Rule 506(b)** — Allows unlimited capital from unlimited accredited + up to 35 sophisticated non-accredited. **No general solicitation**.
- **Rule 506(c)** — permits general solicitation, but **all** purchasers must be accredited.
- **Form D** — must be filed electronically with SEC **within 15 days** of first sale.
- **NSMIA / Securities Act § 18** — federal preemption: states require notice filings only.

## 6. TRUE JV vs. REG D 506(b) SYNDICATION — SIDE-BY-SIDE

| Feature | True JV (not a security) | Reg D 506(b) Syndication (a security) |
|---|---|---|
| Member role | Each member has defined operational duties | Investors are passive; sponsor runs the deal |
| Management | Member-managed OR manager-managed with broad reserved matters | Manager-managed; sponsor has near-total discretion |
| Information rights | Books, records, financials available on demand | Quarterly/annual reports; limited inspection rights |
| Voting | Major decisions require unanimous or supermajority member consent | Limited voting — typically only on extraordinary matters |
| Removal of manager | Members can remove the managing member with cause | Sponsor removable only for fraud/gross negligence |
| Economics | Pro-rata to capital, with sweat equity allocated by role | Waterfall: pref to LPs, then promote to GP |
| Sponsor compensation | Capped, market-rate, disclosed | Acquisition fee, asset-management fee, disposition fee, refinancing fee, promote |
| How members are sourced | Pre-existing relationships; members are working partners | Marketed to investor lists; capital-raisers/broker-dealers |
| Solicitation | Private among partners | 506(b) prohibits general solicitation; 506(c) allows it |
| Disclosure document | Operating agreement and term sheet | Private Placement Memorandum (PPM) |
| Form D filing | None required | Required within 15 days of first sale |
| Bad actor check | None required | Rule 506(d) bad-actor disqualifications apply |
| State filings | None for federal purposes | Notice filings + fees in each state of purchaser residence |
| Right to dissolve / withdraw | Members typically have statutory and contractual exit rights | Lock-up; investors generally cannot withdraw |
| Liability | Members are liable as partners; PG common | LPs have limited liability and contribute capital only |

## 7. OPERATING AGREEMENT PROVISIONS THAT ANCHOR JV STATUS — CHECKLIST

| Provision | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Member-managed structure** (or manager-managed with extensive reserved matters) | Defeats Williamson Factor 1 |
| **Reserved matters requiring unanimous or supermajority consent**: budget approval, capital calls, debt > $X, sale or refinance, hiring/firing key vendors, change of strategy, admission of new members, distributions | Williamson Factor 1 |
| **Defined active duties and deliverables for each member by role**, with quarterly deliverables | Establishes *each* member is contributing "essential managerial effort." Defeats Howey prong 4 and Williamson Factors 2–3. |
| **Information rights**: real-time access to bank accounts, books, contracts, financials | Defeats Williamson Factor 2 |
| **Right to remove and replace the managing member** by majority vote | Defeats Williamson Factor 3 |
| **No single-person bottleneck**: cross-training, documented processes, succession plan | Williamson Factor 3 |
| **Pro-rata or capped sponsor compensation** | A waterfall promote signals sponsor-investor dynamic |
| **Personal guarantees** from active members | Strong evidence of active participation |
| **Capital-call mechanics with member-level approval** | Members "control" their continuing participation |
| **Restrictions on transfer + ROFR** | Confirms closely-held working partnership |
| **Buy-sell mechanics** | Members have practical exit rights |
| **Right to dissolve** under state LLC act | Members can terminate the venture |
| **Regular member meetings** with documented minutes and votes | Builds factual record — critical for *Merchant Capital*-style scrutiny |
| **Representations from each member** of: experience, review of OA and business plan, negotiated terms, intent to actively participate | Defeats Williamson Factor 2 |
| **No general solicitation**; only pre-existing relationships | Avoids worst Howey-style red flag |
| **Acknowledgment that interest is not a security** AND rep that member is accredited or sophisticated | Belt-and-suspenders |

## 8. THE DANGER ZONE — RED FLAGS THAT FLIP A JV INTO A SECURITY

1. **Promoter dominance**. One member writes the OA, picks the deal, controls the bank account.
2. **Non-replaceable expertise**. If only the Operating Member knows how to actually execute.
3. **Disproportionate economics for "passive-looking" members**.
4. **Acquisition fees, promotes, asset-management fees, disposition fees** that resemble syndication compensation. *Long v. Shultz Cattle* — management fee was a key fact pointing to security. A $97K acquisition fee on a $1.9M deal (~5.1%) is at the high end of market for syndication.
5. **Members marketed to via general solicitation**.
6. **The "capital-raising via personal network" 15% slice**. If members receive equity *for raising capital from others*, those raised-capital members are by definition passive investors recruited by an intermediary — and the intermediary may need to be a **registered broker-dealer** under Securities Exchange Act § 15(a). **This is the single largest legal risk in your stack as described.**
7. **Members who are not accredited and not sophisticated**.
8. **Pre-printed "subscription documents," PPMs, or syndication-style marketing decks**.
9. **No member meetings, no votes, no minutes**.
10. **Cross-deals with the same cast of characters**. *Hocking v. Dubois*, 885 F.2d 1449 (9th Cir. 1989) (en banc).

## 9. ACQUISITION FEES IN A TRUE JV

**Question**: Is a $97K acquisition fee paid to organizers consistent with a true JV?

**Doctrine**:
- *Long v. Shultz Cattle Co.* and *Merchant Capital* both treat **per-deal compensation flowing to the organizer** as evidence of a sponsor/investor (i.e., security) dynamic.
- A **single, capped, market-rate, disclosed fee that compensates an active role** is more defensible than a recurring fee or a promote.

**Practical guidance**:
- $97K on $1.9M = **5.1% of purchase price**, or **~14.9% of the $650K equity raised**. That is at the very high end of market and looks like a syndication acquisition fee.
- A defensible alternative: characterize the $97K as **sweat-equity contribution credit** (i.e., recognized as additional capital account credit to the active members who did the acquisition work, payable on a true-up rather than as a cash fee at closing).
- If a cash fee is paid, document the work product.
- **Disclose** the fee to all members in the OA itself.
- **Pro-rate** any economic benefit consistent with the 50/15/35 split.

## 10. HOW TO DOCUMENT ACTIVE PARTICIPATION

**Pre-closing**: Engagement letters, resumes/bios, underwriting model with each member's input, negotiation history of the OA, background checks and accreditation/sophistication questionnaires.

**At closing**: Signed OA with detailed reserved-matters list and member roles, signed PG documents, signed acknowledgment that interest is not intended to be a security, capitalization table.

**Operational (ongoing)**: Monthly or quarterly member meetings with **documented minutes and votes**, written budget approvals, written approvals for any reserved matter, quarterly financials distributed to all members, time logs for active members documenting actual work, K-1s, annual confirmation by each member of continuing active role.

**Defensive documentation**: A short legal memo explaining the no-security analysis at the time of formation, periodic re-confirmation that no member's role has gone passive.

---

## 11. STATE LAW OVERLAY

- **Blue Sky Laws**: applies if the LLC interest is a security under state law. Most states track federal Howey/Williamson reasoning.
- **Anti-fraud rules apply regardless of security characterization**.
- **Uniform Limited Liability Company Act**: most states have adopted some form.
- **Broker-Dealer Registration**: if any member receives transaction-based compensation (the 15% "raise" slice) for soliciting other investors and that capital is raised in connection with a security, that member may be acting as an unregistered broker-dealer in violation of Exchange Act § 15(a). This risk is independent of the Howey analysis. **Resolve this before closing.**

## CLOSING RECOMMENDATION

The structure as described — 6–8 members, every member with a defined operational role, member-managed (or manager-managed with broad reserved matters), and personal guarantees — is the *paradigm* fact pattern that courts and the SEC treat as a true JV.

**Three things will make or break this analysis**:

1. **Restructure or eliminate the 15% "capital-raising via personal network" slice** — it introduces broker-dealer and Howey passivity risk. Convert it to recognition of pre-closing acquisition/diligence work or remove it.
2. **Cap or convert the $97K acquisition fee** into a capital-account credit allocated to the active members who did the acquisition work, rather than a cash fee.
3. **Operate the JV as a JV**: monthly meetings, votes, minutes, financials shared, every member visibly performing their role.

If the deal cannot tolerate these adjustments, treat the offering as a Rule 506(b) syndication, file Form D, and accept the regulatory cost.
